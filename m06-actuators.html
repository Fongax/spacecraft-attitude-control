    Reaction torque and momentum exchange

    The project treated the actuator as a box that delivers whatever body torque the controller asks for, up to ±0.003 N m on each axis Project. Inside that box sits a reaction wheel: a flywheel on an electric motor. The motor cannot push on empty space. It pushes on the wheel, and the wheel pushes back on the spacecraft through the motor's mounting: spin the wheel up one way and the body turns the other way.

    That is momentum exchange. The total angular momentum of body plus wheels, \(H\), can only be changed by external torques such as the disturbance \(\tau_d\); the motor's torque is internal and only moves momentum between the two:

    \[ H=J\omega+h_w,\qquad \tau_c=-\dot h_w \]

    Here \(h_w\) is the wheels' momentum in N m s and \(\tau_c\) the control torque on the body. Putting the wheels into Euler's equation adds one term:

    \[ J\dot\omega+\omega\times\big(J\omega+h_w\big)=-\dot h_w+\tau_d \]

    The project modelled the commanded body torque \(\tau_c=-\dot h_w\) and left out the wheel gyroscopic term \(\omega\times h_w\). Adding it, with each wheel's speed as an extra state, is the natural next step Extension. Angular momentum and impulse–momentum are typical content of a kinematics-and-dynamics subject Typical content.

    The same principle runs through aviation. A helicopter's main rotor drives the fuselage round the other way, which is why it needs a tail rotor, and a multirotor drone yaws by speeding up the rotors that spin one way and slowing the others; torque reaction and multirotor control are typical content of rotary-wing and remotely piloted aircraft subjects Typical content. Gyroscopic instruments, which sense rotation through a spinning rotor's rigidity in space and its precession, are typical content of a light-aircraft systems subject Typical content. A reaction wheel shares the spinning rotor but works differently: it turns the body by speeding up or slowing down (\(\tau_c=-\dot h_w\)), while the actuator that uses precession is the control moment gyro.

    The ±3 mN m box

    The torque limit is small, and it sets the pace of everything. Dividing by the inertias gives the largest angular acceleration each axis can reach:

    \[ \alpha_{max}=\frac{\tau_{max}}{J_i}=0.086,\ 0.075,\ 0.120\ \text{rad/s}^2\quad(x,\ y,\ z) \]

    Derived from \(\tau_{max}=0.003\) N m and \(J=\operatorname{diag}(0.035,\,0.040,\,0.025)\) kg m².

    So the minimum time to brake a rate \(\omega_0\) is \(t=J\omega_0/\tau_{max}\): 1.4 s from the 6 deg/s safe-mode threshold on the y axis, and about 3 s from 13 deg/s Derived, the kind of momentum problem that is typical content of a first-year physics subject Typical content.

    Where does the number come from physically? A DC or brushless motor makes torque in proportion to current, \(\tau=k_M I\), with the torque constant \(k_M\) in N m/A. With an illustrative \(k_M=6\) mN m/A Illustrative, 3 mN m needs 0.5 A. The box is really a current limit set by the drive electronics and the power bus.

    Why is it fair to treat the wheel as an ideal torque source that delivers the command instantly? The motor winding is an R–L circuit, and its current, so its torque, follows a command with the time constant \(L/R\). With an illustrative 0.5 mH and 4 Ω that is about 0.125 ms Illustrative, while the attitude loop moves on time scales of \(J/K_d\approx 1\) s. Four orders of magnitude apart, the fast loop can be treated as instantaneous. The Electromechanics notes teach exactly this time-constant reasoning (63% after one \(L/R\), 99% after five). In software the same box appears once more: a torque command stored as a saturating fixed-point number must clip at its limits rather than wrap around from the largest positive value to the most negative, a number-format issue that is typical content of a computer-architecture subject Typical content.

    Inside the wheel motor

    A spinning motor is also a generator: its rotation induces a back-EMF \(k_e\omega_w\) that opposes the supply. In SI units the back-EMF constant \(k_e\) (V s/rad) equals the torque constant \(k_M\) (N m/A). The winding then obeys

    \[ V=IR+k_e\,\omega_w,\qquad \tau=k_M I\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_w=\frac{V}{k_e}\Big(1-\frac{\tau}{\tau_S}\Big),\quad \tau_S=\frac{k_MV}{R} \]

    This is the straight speed–torque line \(n=n_0(1-T/T_S)\) of the Electromechanics notes. Take an illustrative wheel motor with \(V_{bus}=8\) V, \(R=4\) Ω and \(k_M=0.006\) Illustrative. At stall it draws 2 A and makes 12 mN m; with no load it spins up to 1333 rad/s Illustrative. The drive also limits the current, here to 0.5 A, so the torque actually available at speed \(\omega_w\) is

    \[ \tau_{max}(\omega_w)=\min\Big(k_M I_{lim},\ \frac{k_M\,(V_{bus}-k_e\,\omega_w)}{R}\Big) \]

    The full 3 mN m is available only up to 1000 rad/s, about 9550 rpm Illustrative; above that, back-EMF eats the voltage headroom and the torque falls along the line. The project's constant ±3 mN m box is the left-hand, current-limited part of this envelope. At that corner the power balance is

    \[ VI=I^2R+\tau\,\omega_w:\qquad 4\ \text{W}=1\ \text{W}+3\ \text{W},\qquad \eta=75\% \]

    The powers and the efficiency are Illustrative: 8 V × 0.5 A in, \((0.5\ \text{A})^2\times4\ \Omega\) lost, 3 mN m × 1000 rad/s out.

    The 1 W lost in the winding becomes heat that has to leave a vacuum-sealed wheel. Shaft power \(P=T\omega\), here 1.9 W at 3 mN m and 6000 rpm Illustrative, is typical content of a mechanics-of-materials subject Typical content.

    Real wheels use brushless DC motors. Brushes wear and arc, and graphite lubricates poorly in vacuum; electronic commutation from Hall sensors avoids both, as the Electromechanics notes explain. A wheel's speed and torque signs are independent, so the drive must work in all four quadrants. When the torque opposes the speed, braking a spinning wheel, the machine runs as a generator and returns energy to the bus (regenerative braking), which the power system must be able to absorb. Brushless motors and their controllers are also typical content of a remotely piloted aircraft subject Typical content.

    Momentum budget and unloading

    A wheel can absorb momentum only up to its maximum speed, \(h_{max}=I_w\,\omega_{w,max}\). That makes momentum a budget, like fuel. A detumble is a one-off withdrawal: the body's momentum at the 6 deg/s safe-mode threshold about y is \(J_y\omega=0.0042\) N m s, and at the 15 deg/s safety limit 0.0105 N m s Derived.

    A constant disturbance is a steady drain. To hold attitude, the controller's integral action settles at \(\tau_c=-\tau_d\), so the wheels absorb the disturbance's impulse and their momentum grows linearly:

    \[ h_w(t)=\tau_d\,t,\qquad t_{sat}=\frac{h_{max}}{|\tau_d|} \]

    At 2×10⁻⁵ N m that is 0.108 N m s over a 90-minute orbit Derived, and a wheel with \(h_{max}=0.01\) N m s saturates in 500 s Illustrative. The project's torque-only actuator model cannot show this at all; it needs wheel speed as a state. Pilots plan fuel with reserves and a point of no return, typical content of aircraft performance and planning subjects Typical content; a momentum budget has the same shape, with \(h_{max}/|\tau_d|\) as the "momentum endurance".

    The budget is topped up by momentum unloading with an external torque. A magnetorquer is a coil whose magnetic dipole \(m\) (A m²) reacts against the Earth's field \(B\) (T):

    \[ \tau=m\times B,\qquad |m|\ \ge\ \frac{|\tau_d|}{|B|}\approx 0.67\ \text{A m}^2\ \ \text{at}\ 30\ \mu\text{T} \]

    The field strength is Illustrative; the Electromechanics notes cover the coil-torque law and the Earth's field magnitude.

    The cross product hides a catch: the torque is always perpendicular to \(B\), so there is no torque along the field. Momentum along the field direction can only be dumped later, after the craft's orbit has turned the field. The widget below treats the momentum as lying across the field.

    Clipping vs scaling

    When the controller asks for more than ±3 mN m on some axis, something has to give. The project saturates each axis on its own Project; the site also offers a direction-preserving alternative Site option:

    \[ \tau_{\text{clip},i}=\operatorname{clamp}\big(\tau_i,\,-\tau_{max},\,\tau_{max}\big),\qquad \tau_\text{scale}=\tau\cdot\min\Big(1,\ \frac{\tau_{max}}{\max_i|\tau_i|}\Big) \]

    Clipping is the nearest feasible torque: because the box's constraints are separate per axis, clamping each component is exactly the Euclidean projection onto the box, a separable greedy step of the kind the Algorithms notes discuss. But it rotates the vector. A command of \([6,\,1,\,0]\) mN m clips to \([3,\,1,\,0]\), and its direction moves from 9.5° to 18.4° off the x axis Derived: the craft turns about an axis the controller did not ask for. Scaling keeps the direction, \([3,\,0.5,\,0]\), at the price of delivering less torque on the axes that were within limits.

    A single wheel produces a pure couple on the body. Real spacecraft often carry four wheels in a pyramid, and the body torque is then the vector resultant \(A\,\tau_\text{wheels}\) of the four, so allocation becomes a matrix problem with a spare degree of freedom; couples and resultants are typical content of an engineering statics subject Typical content.

    Non-idealities

    A real wheel delivers the command minus a few unwanted torques. The largest is bearing friction: a Coulomb part that flips with the direction of rotation, a viscous part that grows with speed, and a Stribeck rise at low speed, where the lubricant film is thinnest and the bearing runs in the boundary regime. A common model is

    \[ \tau_f(\omega_w)=\operatorname{sign}(\omega_w)\Big(T_c+(T_s-T_c)\,e^{-(\omega_w/\omega_s)^2}\Big)+c_v\,\omega_w \]

    As the wheel speed passes through zero the friction torque jumps from \(-T_s\) to \(+T_s\). That step is the reaction-wheel zero-crossing disturbance, often much larger than the environmental disturbance; operators bias wheel speeds away from zero or feed friction forward to avoid it. The Design of Machine Elements notes describe the lubrication regimes along the Stribeck curve, and viscous friction is typical content of a fluid-mechanics subject Typical content.

    Rotor imbalance is a mass offset \(U\) (kg m) that produces a force rotating with the wheel, \(F=U\omega^2\): 0.04 N at 6000 rpm for 0.1 g mm Illustrative. It shakes the body at the wheel frequency, the micro-vibration that limits fine pointing. Density variation from casting porosity is one source, and the Manufacturing Technology notes cover casting defects in detail. Misalignment by an angle \(\delta\) leaks \(\tau\sin\delta\) into a neighbouring axis: 1° gives about 1.7% cross-coupling Derived, which is why machining tolerances on wheel mounts matter.

    Hardware extension

    Beyond the original project scope Extension

    The project stopped at a torque box. This section sketches the hardware behind it; every number here is Illustrative.

    Bearings. A wheel runs for years on a preloaded pair of angular-contact ball bearings: preload makes the shaft stiff and quiet but adds friction and heat. Bearing life is a statistic, not a date. The L10 life is the life that 90% of a batch reaches, and a Weibull distribution describes the rest; the Design of Machine Elements notes cover L10 life, Weibull reliability and preload, along with the rule that one bearing locates while the other floats to allow for thermal growth.

    Vacuum. Lubricants evaporate and polymers outgas in vacuum, so wheel bearings use low-vapour-pressure oils in sealed housings. Choosing rotor, mount and lubricant materials, with their densities, strengths and thermal properties, draws on the material classes that are typical content of an engineering materials subject Typical content.

    Rotor strength. A thin rim carries a hoop stress \(\sigma=\rho\,\omega^2r^2\). For a steel rim of 40 mm radius at 1000 rad/s that is about 12 MPa Illustrative, a small fraction of a structural metal's yield strength: small wheels are limited by their motor and bearings, not by rotor strength. Hoop stress in thin-walled vessels, the same formula's static cousin, is typical content of a second mechanics-of-materials subject Typical content.

    Heat. With no air there is no convection: the motor's \(I^2R\) loss can leave only by conduction into the structure and by radiation, and the lubricant's viscosity rises in the cold. Conduction and radiation are typical content of an engineering thermodynamics subject, and a radiative energy balance, which gives a first-cut equilibrium temperature of about 278 K, of an atmospheric science subject Typical content.

    Manufacturing. Material and process variation make a real spacecraft's inertia uncertain by a few per cent; process selection and quality control are typical content of a materials-and-manufacturing subject Typical content. That is one more reason the verification in module 09 samples many cases instead of trusting one nominal model.
