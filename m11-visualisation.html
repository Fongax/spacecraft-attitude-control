Visualisation and UI

How four numbers become a moving spacecraft on a screen, how a recorded run is played back smoothly, and how plots and mode displays let a person check requirements, and predict what the automation will do next, at a glance.

By the end you can…

    From quaternion to pixels

    The project animates its results: a simplified bus with solar panels and a body-fixed boresight axis turns relative to an inertial frame, and the geometry is "representative rather than CAD-accurate" Project. Two of its functions carry the whole idea. quat_to_dcm turns the attitude quaternion into a direction cosine matrix, and draw_spacecraft_frame draws or updates the model Project. This site does the same on a plain canvas, with a box bus of 0.212 × 0.173 × 0.274 m, the uniform 4 kg box whose inertia matches the project's \(J\) to within 0.2% Derived.

    Every frame runs the same pipeline. Convert \(q\) to a rotation matrix once; rotate each body-frame vertex into the inertial frame; project it onto the screen; sort the faces from far to near; draw them in that order so near faces paint over far ones.

    \[ R(q)=(q_0^2-q_v^{\top}q_v)\,I+2\,q_vq_v^{\top}+2\,q_0[q_v\times],\qquad v_I=R\,v_B,\qquad (x_s,\ y_s,\ d)=(c_x+s\,v_I\!\cdot\!\hat r,\ \ c_y-s\,v_I\!\cdot\!\hat u,\ \ v_I\!\cdot\!\hat d) \]

    \(\hat r\), \(\hat u\) and \(\hat d\) are the camera's right, up and toward-viewer directions; \(s\) is the pixel scale. The depth \(d\) is what the face sort uses.

    Why convert once? The Algorithms notes count multiplications to choose the evaluation order of a matrix chain; applied here, rotating a vertex with the quaternion "sandwich" \(q\otimes v\otimes q^{*}\) costs two Hamilton products of 16 multiplications each, while applying a matrix costs 9. And composing two rotations is itself a matrix-chain question:

    \[ (R_1R_2)\,V:\ 27+9N\qquad\text{against}\qquad R_1(R_2V):\ 18N\qquad (N=1000:\ 9027\ \text{vs}\ 18\,000) \]

    A canvas has no depth buffer, so faces are drawn back to front: the painter's algorithm. Between two frames the attitude barely changes, so last frame's order is almost sorted, and the same notes show that insertion sort costs \((n-1)\) plus the number of inversions: nearly linear when the input is nearly sorted. The simplified bus could be replaced by exported CAD geometry, every vertex still rotated by the quaternion-derived matrix each frame; producing that geometry is typical content of a digital design and modelling subject Typical content.

    Inertial vs body frames on screen

    The project's animation shows the spacecraft body frame rotating relative to an inertial frame Project, and that single design choice does a lot of teaching. Drawing both sets of axes makes the kinematics visible: the inertial triad stays put, the body triad turns with the bus, and the angular velocity is an arrow fixed in neither. Rotating frames and the transport theorem are typical content of a kinematics-and-dynamics subject Typical content:

    \[ \left.\frac{dA}{dt}\right|_{I}=\left.\frac{dA}{dt}\right|_{B}+\omega\times A \]

    That one equation is why \(\omega\) is expressed in the body frame, and where the gyroscopic term \(\omega\times J\omega\) in Euler's equation comes from (module 03).

    The UI has a second frame question to answer. People think in roll, pitch and yaw, so the project's interface takes the initial attitude as three Euler angles Project, but it simulates with quaternions. The reason is the singularity in the Euler-angle rates, which divide by \(\cos\theta\):

    \[ \dot\psi=\frac{q\sin\phi+r\cos\phi}{\cos\theta}\ \to\ \infty\quad\text{as}\quad\theta\to\pm90^{\circ} \]

    This gimbal lock argument, and the Euler-angle kinematics behind it, are typical content of an aerodynamics subject that covers aircraft rigid-body equations Typical content. Euler angles are a good input and display format and a poor state; the screen can show roll, pitch and yaw while the engine never stores them.

    Plots that check requirements

    A result plot should answer a question without a calculator. The trick is to draw the requirement on the plot: the rate norm with lines at 0.5 and 6 deg/s (the safe-mode thresholds) and 15 deg/s (REQ-S1); the attitude error with 2° (REQ-F2) and 4° (the return guard); each torque axis with ±3 mN m (REQ-S2) Project. Add the mode as coloured bands underneath and the eye checks a run in a second: a curve that crosses a red line is a failed requirement. Plots with threshold lines, and a slider interface that mirrors the project's, are typical content of a programming-for-engineers subject Typical content.

    Colour alone is not enough: many readers see red and green poorly, and printouts are often grey. Every series on this site therefore also has its own dash pattern and label, modes carry text names, and pass and fail badges carry icons. The figure below lets you check that claim by switching the colour off.

    Plots are also data structures. The Data Structures notes teach the sparse table, which answers range-maximum queries on static data in O(1). Applied here: once a run is finished its log never changes, so a sparse table answers "what was the peak rate in the window I dragged?", and prefix sums give the windowed control effort:

    \[ \max_{[l,r]}=\max\!\big(M_j[l],\ M_j[r-2^{j}+1]\big),\ \ j=\lfloor\log_2(r-l+1)\rfloor,\qquad E(t_1,t_2)=P[k_2]-P[k_1] \]

    The overlap trick works for a maximum, because counting a sample twice changes nothing, but would silently double-count effort, which is why the two queries need different structures. Annotated step-response, speed–torque and phasor diagrams in the Electromechanics notes are good models for result plots, and the chart-reading culture of the Design of Machine Elements notes (S-N curves, Goodman diagrams) suits interactive plots. Low-dimensional maps of Monte Carlo trials need care: the Unsupervised and Reinforcement Learning notes found that t-SNE invents clusters in noise, that UMAP's claimed advantage in global structure did not reproduce, and that PCA preserves global structure better. Plots exist to make results understandable to others, a point of technical communication that is typical content of an engineering design practice subject Typical content.

    Time, interpolation and binary search

    The project's interface plays its animation at a chosen stride and speed Project, so the display time almost never lands exactly on a logged sample. Two questions follow: which samples bracket the display time, and what attitude lies between them?

    The first is a search. The time column is sorted, so binary search finds the bracketing interval in \(\lceil\log_2 n\rceil\) steps: 14 steps for the 12,001 samples of a 120 s run at 0.01 s Derived, done in place on the indices with no copying: the in-place binary search of the Computing Algorithms notes.

    The second is interpolation, and attitude is not an ordinary number. Interpolating Euler angles component by component gives a wobbling, non-uniform path; interpolating quaternion components linearly leaves the unit sphere. The right tool is slerp, which moves at constant angular speed along the great arc between the two samples:

    \[ \mathrm{slerp}(q_a,q_b;s)=\frac{\sin\big((1-s)\Omega\big)}{\sin\Omega}\,q_a+\frac{\sin(s\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\,q_b,\qquad \cos\Omega=q_a\!\cdot q_b\ \ (\text{flip }q_b\text{ if negative}) \]

    For small steps, normalised linear interpolation ("nlerp": blend, then divide by the norm) follows almost the same path and is cheaper. Flipping \(q_b\) when the dot product is negative matters: \(q\) and \(-q\) are the same attitude, and without the flip the interpolation takes the long way round. This site's ADCS.sim.sampleAt does exactly this: binary search, then slerp for the attitude and linear interpolation for everything else.

    One model, many views

    The project shipped a modern interface and a legacy one for older installations, both over the same simulation functions, and a batch script that calls those functions with no interface at all Project. That is the payoff of model–view–controller separation, which the Software Engineering and Architecture notes describe: the model (dynamics, controllers, mode logic, result tables) knows nothing about the views (plots, animation, CSV and video export), and the controller (the UI callbacks for initial attitude, rates, controller type, fault window and playback stride) only passes inputs in. The notes' practical test translates directly: if checking the safe-mode logic needs the UI open, the separation has failed. Here, the batch run and the Monte Carlo campaign call the core headlessly, and the Python mirror checks the CSV files the core writes. A web front end driving the same simulation core is the same idea, and is typical content of a software-projects subject Typical content.

    The other half of the lesson is the event loop. A user interface runs its callbacks on one thread, and the Systems Programming and Memory notes show the consequence: code between two yields runs to completion, so a blocking call stalls everything. A 60 s simulation inside a button callback freezes the window. The fix is to run the model, then animate from a timer, or to break long work into chunks. This site's Monte Carlo runner does the second, about 12 ms of trials at a time, so the page stays responsive.

    Views can also be fed by events. Publish a ModeChanged{from, to, t, reason, values} record at each transition and let the logger, telemetry, timeline and annunciator subscribe; the mode timeline is then a fold over the events, and a replayed event log rebuilds it exactly. For streaming live attitude to a dashboard, the Networking notes measured 6 bytes of WebSocket framing per message against about 2,818 bytes for an HTTP poll, and note that one-way server events are enough for telemetry.

    Mode annunciation

    Automation that changes mode on its own creates a new failure: the person watching no longer knows what it is doing. Mode confusion, where the operator's mental model of the mode differs from the real one, and the "automation surprise" it causes are typical content of a human-factors-for-pilots subject Typical content. The design rules that follow are simple to state:

    • Always show the current mode, large, with its name, not only a colour.
    • Show why it changed: which guard fired, with the value that fired it.
    • Show what the next change needs: each exit guard as a lamp, and the remaining dwell before a return to NOMINAL as a progress bar.

    With the project's guards (enter detumble above 6 deg/s or after a 0.5 s fault; leave after 2 s below 0.5 deg/s; return when the fault is clear, the rate is below 0.5 deg/s, the error is below 4° and 8 s have passed) Project, a display that shows those four lamps makes the machine predictable: anyone can say what it will do next. The forbidden transitions help too, because there are fewer surprises to explain. The site's annunciator, ADCS.ui.modeAnnunciator, follows these rules, and the lab below tests them on you.

    The lab uses "predict, then reveal": commit to an answer before seeing the outcome. Peer Instruction, with its concept question, vote and re-vote, is typical content of an aviation science subject Typical content, and it suits animations well because a prediction makes the surprise visible.

    Performance budget

    Can a browser really run the project's verification? RK4 evaluates the dynamics four times per step, so counting the work as the Algorithms notes teach gives, for the Monte Carlo campaign:

    \[ N_{eval}=4\cdot\frac{T}{\Delta t}\cdot n=4\cdot\frac{60\ \text{s}}{0.01\ \text{s}}\cdot 60\approx1.44\times10^{6} \]

    About 1.4 million derivative evaluations, each a few dozen multiplications: well under a few seconds of JavaScript, if the work is chunked so the page never freezes. This site's 120 s campaign does twice that and runs in slices of about 12 ms Site default.

    Drawing is the other budget. A 120 s run has 12,001 samples per channel Derived, but a plot is perhaps 600 pixels wide, so drawing every sample wastes time and adds nothing. Decimate to the pixel width, keeping each column's minimum and maximum so that short spikes, such as a one-sample threshold crossing, never vanish. Plot redraws on this site take a few milliseconds, well inside the 16 ms of a 60 Hz frame.
