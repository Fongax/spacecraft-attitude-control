    The test matrix

    A requirement is only as good as the evidence that it is met. The project's test plan turns its five requirements into six tests, each with a scenario and an expected result written before anything is run Project. T01 and T02 check pointing (REQ-F2) with each nominal controller. T03 is the safe-mode upset with a temporary fault, and it checks three requirements at once: F1, S1 and S2. T04 is the 60-trial Monte Carlo campaign behind the statistical requirement L1. T05 and T06 are sweeps rather than scenarios: the largest rate and the largest torque across every run.

    The link from requirements to tests is the traceability built in module 01. Two checks keep it honest: every requirement has at least one test (nothing is unverified), and every test serves at least one requirement (nothing is an orphan). In the Data Structures notes' terms the trace is an incidence matrix, and T03 is a hyperedge: it verifies {F1, S1, S2} together in one scenario, which a list of pairs would lose.     Metrics from logs

    A pass or a fail is a summary; the metrics behind it come from the logged time series. The project defines them precisely Project, and the precision matters.

    Settling time is "the first time after which attitude error < 2° and angular rate < 0.5 deg/s remain satisfied". That is the last entry into the band, not the first: a response that dips in and bounces out has not settled. Computed naively, by testing every candidate start forward, it costs O(n²). Scanning backward once from the end finds the last violating sample \(k^*\) in O(n):

    \[ k^*=\max\{k:\ \lnot(e_k<2^\circ\land\lVert\omega_k\rVert<0.5^\circ/\text{s})\},\qquad t_s=t_{k^*+1} \]

    Control effort is the integral of the squared torque magnitude, which the site integrates by the trapezoid rule over the logged samples, in N² m² s:

    \[ E=\int_0^T\lVert\tau\rVert^2\,dt\ \approx\ \sum_k\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\big(\lVert\tau_k\rVert^2+\lVert\tau_{k+1}\rVert^2\big) \]

    Safety margin is the distance from the 15 deg/s limit, \(15-\max_t\lVert\omega\rVert\), and maximum torque is the largest actuator command after saturation. The margin has a precise meaning in signal temporal logic: the robustness of the property "always \(\lVert\omega\rVert\le15\) deg/s" is the smallest distance to violation over the whole run,

    \[ \rho\big(\mathbf G\,(\lVert\omega\rVert\le15)\big)=\min_t\big(15-\lVert\omega(t)\rVert\big)=15-\max_t\lVert\omega(t)\rVert \]

    so the project's worst Monte Carlo case gives a robustness of \(15-13.22=1.78\) deg/s Project. A positive robustness means the property holds, and its size says how much worse things could get before it fails. The Electromechanics notes define settling time the same way, as the last entry into a ±2% or ±5% band after which the output stays inside it, and the complexity analysis taught in the Computing Algorithms notes shows why the backward scan is O(n) where the naive search is O(n²).     The Monte Carlo campaign

    T03 tests one upset. REQ-L1 asks about upsets in general, "at least 95% of Monte Carlo trials", so the project ran a Monte Carlo campaign Project:

    • 60 trials;
    • initial attitude: a random axis with an angle of up to 120°;
    • initial rate: each axis uniform in −8 to 8 deg/s;
    • disturbance torque: each axis uniform in ±2×10⁻⁵ N m;
    • PID nominal control with the safe-mode logic switched on.

    The pass rule for L1 needs stating, because it is an interpretation: a trial passes if it never needed detumbling (it began below the detumble threshold) or if every safe-mode entry reached \(\lVert\omega\rVert<0.5\) deg/s within 60 s of the entry Project. Separately, a trial is finally stable if it ends in NOMINAL with error below 2° and rate below 0.5 deg/s, and safe if it never exceeds 15 deg/s or the torque limit.

    The project reported a 100% detumble pass rate, 100% final stability, 100% safety and a worst rate of 13.22 deg/s Project. This site, with seed 42 and its own gains, gets the same three verdicts, 60/60 each, with a worst rate of 12.47 deg/s, 54 safe-mode entries in total, a longest detumble of 2.62 s and the last return to NOMINAL by 52.8 s Derived. The numbers differ in detail because the gains and the random draws differ; the verdicts agree.

    Robust design calls the randomised inputs noise factors: conditions the design cannot choose but must pass across. That idea is typical content of a mechanical engineering design subject Typical content, and the solar-cycle variation of thermospheric density, typical content of an atmospheric science subject, is a physical reason to randomise the disturbance at all Typical content. Natural extensions would randomise more: the inertia (manufacturing variation, say \(J_i\times U(0.9,1.1)\)), actuator misalignment and rotor imbalance Extension. The trials are independent, so the campaign is embarrassingly parallel: the Systems Programming notes' measurements of threads against processes, and their advice to vectorise before parallelising, apply directly. The threads and the mt19937 generator of the Object-Oriented Programming (C++) notes show the safe way to seed it: one stream per trial, and an atomic pass counter.

    Sampling the initial conditions

    How the trials are drawn decides what the campaign tests. Start with the rates. Each axis is uniform in ±8 deg/s, so the largest possible initial rate is the corner of the cube:

    \[ \lVert\omega_0\rVert\le8\sqrt3=13.86\ \text{deg/s}<15\ \text{deg/s} \]

    No trial ever starts above the REQ-S1 limit Derived. So what REQ-S1 tests here is narrower than it looks: that the controller never spins the craft up past 15 deg/s from a start of at most 13.86 deg/s. The worst peak, 12.47 deg/s, is exactly trial 0's initial rate. The controller does spin some trials up: in 11 trials the peak exceeds the initial rate, because the slew from a large attitude error accelerates the craft. In 9 of them, all starting below 6 deg/s, the PID slew crosses the 6 deg/s guard within about 1.3 s and trips safe mode (peaks 6.0–6.3 deg/s), and none of the 11 passes 6.7 deg/s Derived. That is why 54 trials enter safe mode although only 45 start above 6 deg/s. Each per-axis rate has mean 0 and variance \((b-a)^2/12=(16)^2/12\approx21.3\) (deg/s)², the uniform-distribution facts that are typical content of an engineering mathematics subject Typical content.

    The attitude is subtler. "A random axis with an angle up to 120°" read literally, as an angle drawn uniformly in 0 to 120° about a uniformly random axis, is how this site samples it. It is not a uniform sample of orientations. Uniform orientations correspond to uniform unit quaternions on the 3-sphere, and with \(q_0=\cos(\theta/2)\) a change of variables gives the rotation-angle density

    \[ f(\theta)=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\pi},\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi \]

    which is tiny near zero: there are few ways to be almost aligned and many ways to be far off. Restricted to 120°, uniform orientations put only about 1.9% of trials below 30°, while the uniform-angle method puts 25% there Derived. The project's sampling, read this way, over-weights easy, small-angle cases. That does not make the result wrong, but the 100% applies to the sampled distribution, not to "any attitude". Change of variables in multiple integrals is typical content of a second calculus subject Typical content. The Algorithms notes make the same point about shuffling: a naive shuffle is biased, Fisher–Yates is not, and the lesson is to sample uniformly in the right space.     What 60/60 proves

    Sixty passes out of sixty is not "100% reliable". Model each trial as a pass or a fail with some unknown pass probability \(p\); the campaign is then a binomial experiment, and the honest question is how small \(p\) could be and still make 60/60 plausible. The one-sided Clopper–Pearson lower bound \(p_L\) at confidence \(C=1-\alpha\) is the \(p\) at which seeing \(s\) or more passes in \(n\) trials has probability exactly \(\alpha\):

    \[ \sum_{k=s}^{n}\binom nk p_L^{\,k}(1-p_L)^{n-k}=\alpha \]

    With zero failures (\(s=n\)) only the last term survives, \(p_L^{\,n}=\alpha\), so

    \[ p_L=\alpha^{1/n},\qquad 0.05^{1/60}=0.9513 \]

    At 95% confidence, 60/60 shows the pass rate is at least 95.1% Derived. REQ-L1 asks for 95%, so the campaign demonstrates it, but only just. One failure changes the verdict: 59/60 gives a bound of 0.9234, and 58/60 gives 0.8988, both short of 95% even though 59/60 = 98.3% looks comfortable Derived. The rule of three is the quick version: with no failures in n trials, the failure rate is below about \(3/n\) at 95% confidence, here 5%.

    Turn it around. If the true pass rate were exactly 95%, a campaign of 60 would still come out 60/60 with probability \(0.95^{60}=4.6\%\) Derived, and that is why 60/60 is evidence for "at least about 95%" and no more. To demonstrate a reliability \(R\) at confidence \(C\) with zero failures you need

    \[ n=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1-C)}{\ln R}\right\rceil:\qquad 59\ \text{trials for }95\%,\qquad 299\ \text{for }99\% \]

    at 95% confidence Derived. The project's 60 is one more than the minimum. These bounds, the sample-size formula and the binomial model are typical content of a quantitative reasoning subject and of an engineering mathematics subject Typical content. The Algorithms notes' warning fits exactly: testing cannot establish the correctness of a heuristic, and the numbers above say how far it can go. The Design of Machine Elements notes treat bearing life the same way: L10 is the life that 90% of a batch reaches, a percentile claim about a distribution, just as REQ-L1 is a claim about a fraction of trials.

    Worst cases and margins

    A mean answers "how does it usually behave?"; a safety requirement asks "does it ever misbehave?". REQ-S1 says the rate shall never exceed 15 deg/s, so the evidence that matters is the tail: the worst case, and a high percentile with its method stated. Certification works this way. Aircraft performance is planned on the worst credible case, not the average day, and reporting the worst Monte Carlo case (13.22 against 15 deg/s) is the same habit; that framing is typical content of an aircraft performance and planning subject Typical content.

    The project explained its worst cases: they "occur when the initial attitude error is large and the initial angular rate vector excites more than one body axis", with torque saturation active early, so the response is limited by the actuator rather than the gains Project. Explaining the tail is a data-mining task in miniature: each trial is a row, and the classification and association rules listed in the Data Mining notes, applied to (initial angle, |ω₀|, disturbance), can find the regime that produces slow or extreme trials. Clustering the outcomes does the same without labels, and the Unsupervised Learning notes add a warning for anyone choosing a design from such a table: picking the best of several noisy scores is biased upward, the winner's curse.

    A margin is conditional on the envelope that produced it. The worst site trial peaked at 12.47 deg/s because that was its initial rate, and no trial can start above 13.86 deg/s, so the 2.5 deg/s margin is set mostly by the sampling range, not by the controller. The tightest margin on the site comes from no random trial at all: the T02 slew from rest under LQR, with safe mode off, peaks at 14.28 deg/s, a margin of 0.72 deg/s, because the saturated actuator accelerates the craft for longer than the linear design expects (module 05) Derived. That is why T05 sweeps the named scenarios as well as the campaign. The Supervised Learning notes make the general point: the evidence cannot extrapolate beyond its sample, as a random forest cannot predict outside its training range, so a claim about a 12 deg/s-per-axis upset needs new trials. When trials do fail, a structured review helps; tiered accident models that separate the act, its preconditions, supervision and organisation are typical content of an aviation human-factors subject Typical content.     Seeds, reproducibility and leakage

    A campaign is only evidence if it can be repeated. The project's risk register names the risk "results not reproducible" and its mitigation: "fix random seed for Monte Carlo and write all outputs to CSV" Project. This site goes one step further and gives every trial its own seed, derived from the base seed and the trial index, so trial 17 is the same trial whatever else runs and can be replayed alone. The Object-Oriented Programming (C++) notes give the same rule for <random>: draw a true random seed at most once, to choose and log the base seed, never inside the verification loop. The ordered maps and hash functions of the Data Structures notes give the rest: tables written in a deterministic row and column order, so two runs produce identical files, and a hash of the parameters stamped on every results file. Storing one row per trial, written in one transaction per campaign, is the database version.

    The subtler danger is leakage. If the gains are tuned until the 60 seeded trials all pass, and the same 60 trials are then reported as verification, the result is optimistic: the design has been fitted to those draws. The Intelligent Systems notes call mixing validation into training the number-one mistake, and the Supervised Learning notes show how large such leaks can be. The fix is the train/validation/test discipline applied to seed sets: tune on one set, verify once on a fresh one. When comparing two designs, run both on the same seeds (common random numbers), because

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(A-B)=\operatorname{Var}(A)+\operatorname{Var}(B)-2\operatorname{Cov}(A,B) \]

    and positively correlated results make the difference much less noisy. Finally, choose a metric that can tell designs apart. If every reasonable gain passes 60/60, nothing separates them. In the local-search language of the Intelligent Systems notes the pass rate is a plateau: hill climbing over gains stops at once, and two designs that differ tenfold in detumble time look identical. The cure is a finer-grained evaluation, such as the detumble-time distribution or the margin. The optional widget below shows it with the detumble gain.

    What sampling can and cannot show

    "Testing shows the presence of bugs, not their absence." Every campaign on this page samples: it can expose a failure, and with enough clean trials it can bound the failure rate at a stated confidence, but it can never show that no failure exists. That limit is typical content of a logic and automated reasoning subject Typical content, and it is why the project's verification uses two kinds of evidence for two kinds of question.

    Two kinds of evidence, matched to two kinds of question
    QuestionMethodWhat it givesWhat it cannot give
    Can the mode logic ever take a forbidden transition?Exhaustive checking of the finite logic (module 08)Certainty over the abstraction, and the shortest counterexample if it failsAnything outside the abstraction: the continuous dynamics, timing, the real code
    Do the dynamics detumble in time from realistic upsets?Seeded Monte Carlo over the initial conditions (this module)A pass rate with a confidence bound, worst cases, distributionsProof of absence of failures; anything outside the sampled envelope
    Does every logged run respect the rules?Run-time monitor on every log (module 08)A check of each actual run, in O(n)Runs that were never made

    The project's split is the right one: formal reasoning for the small, discrete, safety-critical logic, where exhaustive checking is cheap and complete; statistics for the continuous dynamics, where enumeration is impossible and sampling with stated confidence is the honest alternative; and a monitor on every log to catch what both might miss. Signal temporal logic connects the two worlds: the robustness of a sampled run, such as the 1.78 deg/s S1 margin, is a quantitative version of a logical property Project.
