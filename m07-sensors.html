MODULE 07

Sensors and estimation

Take away the simulator's perfect knowledge, give the controller a gyro and a star tracker instead, and build the filters that turn their imperfect readings into an attitude it can trust.

    Why estimate?

    Every controller in this project so far has been told the truth. At each step the simulation hands the control law the exact quaternion \(q\) and the exact body rate \(\omega\), straight out of the integrator. The project's requirements say so plainly: sensor noise and estimator dynamics were outside the first project scope, and the true simulated state was used for feedback Project. That was a sound choice for a first project. It isolates the questions the project set out to answer: dynamics, control laws, safe-mode logic and verification.

    A real spacecraft never sees its state. It sees measurements. A rate gyro reports a rate that is slightly wrong in a slowly changing way; a star tracker reports an attitude a few times a second and is sometimes blinded by the Sun. Between the measurements and the controller sits an estimator, which keeps a belief about the state and revises it as evidence arrives. The controller then acts on the belief, not on the truth.

    \[ \underbrace{(q,\ \omega)}_{\text{true state}}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{sensors}\ }\ \underbrace{(q_m,\ \omega_m)}_{\text{measurements}}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{estimator}\ }\ \underbrace{(\hat q,\ \hat\omega)}_{\text{belief}}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{control law}\ }\ \tau \]

    The Intelligent Systems notes give the vocabulary for this. An agent perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators, and its agent function maps the whole percept sequence to an action, not just the latest percept. That distinction is the heart of estimation: no single gyro sample tells you the attitude, but the history of samples, combined with an occasional absolute fix, does. In those terms the project assumed a fully observable world; a real attitude system is only partially observable and must carry its own memory.

    People carry an estimator too. The inner ear's semicircular canals sense rotation but stop reporting a steady rotation after some seconds, and the otolith organs cannot tell a tilt from an acceleration. Spatial disorientation in flight comes from exactly these limits, which is why pilots are trained to trust the instruments. The vestibular system as a biological rate sensor is typical content of an aviation-medicine subject Typical content. It makes the point of this module: a rate sensor alone is not enough, and fusing it with an absolute reference is what keeps the estimate honest.

    The rest of the module builds that estimator piece by piece: the sensors (gyros, then absolute sensors), what goes wrong without fusion (drift), two ways to fuse (a complementary filter and a Kalman-style filter derived from Bayes' rule), and finally what happens when noisy estimates meet the safe-mode thresholds of module 08.

    Rate gyros

    A rate gyro measures the body rate \(\omega\) directly, hundreds of times a second, with good short-term precision. Its errors are what make estimation necessary. The standard error model, per axis, is

    \[ \omega_m=\omega+b+n,\qquad \dot b=n_u \]

    where \(b\) is the bias, a slowly wandering offset in rad/s; \(n\) is white rate noise; and \(n_u\) is the small noise that drives the bias's random walk. Integrate \(\omega_m\) alone to get an angle and the bias integrates to a ramp while the white noise integrates to a random walk, so the attitude error grows as

    \[ \delta\theta(t)\approx b\,t+\mathrm{ARW}\sqrt{t} \]

    ARW, the angle random walk, is the white-noise density, quoted in deg/√h. The two terms are equal at \(t=(\mathrm{ARW}/b)^2\). For a 10 deg/h bias, a modest figure for a small-satellite gyro, and an ARW of 0.1 deg/√h Illustrative, that is \((0.1/10)^2\) h \(=10^{-4}\) h, about 0.36 s, so the bias ramp dominates almost at once. Only a very stable gyro is noise-limited for long: with the same ARW, a 0.01 deg/h bias would take 100 h to catch up Derived. The 10 deg/h bias alone carries the estimate past the project's 2° pointing band after \(2^\circ/(10\ \text{deg/h})=0.2\) h \(=720\) s, twelve minutes Derived.

    The simulator's sensor extension is harsher. Its gyro bias is [0.02, −0.015, 0.01] deg/s, which is 72 deg/h on the x axis Extension; unaided, that alone would cross 2° in 100 s Derived. The filters below therefore have to estimate the bias, not just average the noise. Its white noise, σ = 0.01 deg/s per 0.01 s sample, corresponds to an ARW of 0.06 deg/√h Derived.

    Most small-satellite gyros are MEMS devices. A tiny proof mass is driven to vibrate along one axis; when the body rotates, the Coriolis acceleration \(2\,\omega\times v_{rel}\) pushes the mass along a perpendicular axis, where a capacitive pick-off reads the motion. Rotating-frame kinematics and the Coriolis term are typical content of a kinematics and dynamics subject Typical content.

    The samples also have a software path. Before anything else, the analogue-to-digital converter rounds each reading to a step \(\Delta\), adding quantisation noise of variance \(\Delta^2/12\); that model is typical content of a computer architecture subject Typical content. Then the readings must reach the estimator. The single-producer, single-consumer ring buffer of the Data Structures notes is the standard hand-off from a sensor interrupt to the estimator task, and their constant-time running-sum window gives a moving-average prefilter at O(1) per sample Extension.

    Absolute sensors

    A gyro tells you how fast you are turning; an absolute sensor tells you where you are pointing. Three families dominate small spacecraft.

    • Star trackers photograph the sky, match the star pattern against a catalogue and output a full attitude quaternion. They are the most accurate (a few arcseconds is common) but slow (a few to ten updates a second), they need a "lost in space" solution at start-up, and the Sun or the Earth in the field of view blinds them Illustrative.
    • Coarse sun sensors are photodiodes or small solar cells. A cell whose normal \(\hat n\) makes angle \(\theta\) with the Sun direction \(\hat s\) gives a current of roughly \(I=I_0\max(0,\cos\theta)=I_0\max(0,\ \hat n\cdot\hat s)\), so a handful of cells on different faces give the Sun vector in body axes. They are cheap and robust, good to a degree or so Illustrative.
    • Magnetometers measure the local magnetic field vector with Hall-effect elements or magnetoresistive bridges, for comparison with a model of the Earth's field.

    A Sun or field direction is one unit vector, and one direction fixes only two of the three attitude angles: rotation about that direction stays invisible. Two non-parallel directions (the Sun and the field, say) fix the attitude completely. A star tracker sees many stars at once, so it delivers the whole quaternion on its own. Each sensor therefore gives either a vector measurement or a full attitude measurement, and the estimator must know which.

    The sensing elements sit squarely in the Electromechanics notes, which is why this is the one link in this module tagged direct rather than extension: photodiodes, Hall-effect sensors, the Wheatstone bridge, Gray-code absolute encoders, slotted incremental encoders and tachogenerators all appear there. The notes also explain why the project's controllers take rate from a gyro instead of differencing the attitude: a differentiator's gain rises in proportion to frequency, so it amplifies exactly the high-frequency noise you least want in a torque command.

    Accuracy is a budget, not a single number. Mounting misalignment, thermal distortion and sensor noise add up, and the Design of Machine Elements notes give the two ways to add them, as a tolerance stack-up Extension:

    \[ e_{\text{worst}}=\sum_i e_i,\qquad e_{\text{RSS}}=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i e_i^2}\quad\text{(only for independent contributions)} \]

    Sensor mounts in low-expansion materials, and non-magnetic alloys near a magnetometer, are typical content of an engineering materials subject Typical content. Star-tracker image processing (finding star centroids and identifying the pattern) is a natural home for the convolutional networks of the Deep Learning notes, whose translation equivariance suits star fields Extension. And when three redundant sensors vote two out of three, the same notes give the reliability, \(R=3p^2-2p^3\): better than one sensor only when \(p>0.5\), and no help at all if all three fail the same way.

    Dead reckoning and drift

    Integrating a rate to get an angle is dead reckoning. Navigators did it long before gyros: heading, speed and elapsed time give a position, every error in heading or speed accumulates, and the error keeps growing until a fix (a landmark, a radio bearing, a star sight) resets it. Dead reckoning, the 1-in-60 rule and position fixing are typical content of an aviation navigation subject Typical content, and the analogy with gyro-only attitude is exact.

    For attitude, the propagation step is the project's own kinematic equation from module 03, run on measured rates with the bias estimate removed:

    \[ \dot{\hat q}=\tfrac12\,\hat q\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0\\ \omega_m-\hat b\end{bmatrix} \]

    With no absolute fix, nothing ever pulls the estimate back. The bias ramp grows linearly. The random walk grows as \(\sqrt t\), more slowly but without bound, and it remains even if the bias were known perfectly. With a fix every \(N\) seconds the error can no longer grow beyond what accumulates between two fixes, plus the error of the fix itself. In the widget below the fixes go through a small Kalman filter that also learns the bias, so after a few fixes the error between them shrinks to hundredths of a degree.

    Watch the units. A 10 deg/h bias is 2,160 times smaller than the 6 deg/s safe-mode entry threshold, and it is irrelevant to the mode logic. But pointing is an integral, and the integral of a small constant becomes large given enough time: in twelve minutes that bias consumes the whole 2° pointing budget Derived.

    Complementary filter

    The gyro is good over short times and bad over long ones; an absolute sensor is the reverse, noisy from sample to sample but free of drift. A complementary filter takes the high frequencies from one and the low frequencies from the other:

    \[ \hat\theta_k=\alpha\,\big(\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_m\,\Delta t\big)+(1-\alpha)\,\theta_{ref} \]

    Each step it propagates with the gyro, then nudges the result a fraction \(1-\alpha\) of the way toward the reference. The nudges act as a first-order low-pass filter on the reference and a high-pass filter on the gyro, with the crossover time constant

    \[ \tau=\frac{\alpha\,\Delta t}{1-\alpha} \]

    where \(\Delta t\) is the interval between reference updates. Faster than \(\tau\) the filter follows the gyro; slower than \(\tau\) it follows the reference. A large \(\alpha\) trusts the gyro (smooth, but slow to correct drift); a small \(\alpha\) trusts the reference (and follows its noise).

    A light aircraft's attitude indicator does this with hardware. Its gyro holds the horizon over short times, and a slow erection mechanism of pendulous vanes pulls it back toward gravity over minutes. Resetting the heading indicator to the compass every 10 to 15 minutes is the same idea for heading: a bias correction against an absolute reference. These instruments are typical content of a light aircraft systems subject Typical content. The Intelligent Systems notes contain the same filter in another guise: the temporal-difference running average \(\bar x\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\bar x+\alpha x\) is exponential smoothing, a first-order filter with a memory of about \(1/\alpha\) samples Extension.

    A plain complementary filter has one flaw: a constant gyro bias leaves a constant error of about \(b\,\tau\). The fix is a second state, the bias estimate, updated from the residual \(r=\theta_{ref}-\hat\theta\) whenever a reference arrives and subtracted from \(\omega_m\) before integrating:

    \[ \hat b\leftarrow\hat b-k_b\,r \]

    The simulator's complementary option does exactly this on all three axes, with the error quaternion as the residual: \(k_c=0.2\) per star-tracker update, so \(\alpha=0.8\), and \(k_b=0.05\) Extension. At the star tracker's 5 Hz that gives \(\tau=0.8\) s Derived, and the choice \(k_b=k_c^2/(4\Delta t)\) makes the two-state loop critically damped Derived. You can compare a complementary filter with the Kalman filter in the playground two sections below.

    The inner ear makes the case for fusion vividly. The semicircular canals behave roughly like a high-pass filter on the roll rate, \(\omega_{felt}/\omega=\tau s/(\tau s+1)\) with \(\tau\) of several seconds, and a change in rate below about 2 deg/s goes unnoticed. A slow roll into a bank is therefore never felt, and a brisk roll back to level is felt in full, so a pilot who is level feels banked the other way: "the leans". These figures are typical content of an aviation-medicine subject Typical content; the cure is the one this section describes, an absolute reference (the outside horizon or the attitude indicator) fused with the rate sense.

    From Bayes' rule to the Kalman filter

    A complementary filter needs hand-tuned gains. A Kalman filter computes them from the noise models, and its structure falls straight out of Bayes' rule.

    Treat the state \(x_k\) (attitude error and bias) as uncertain and keep a probability distribution over it, the belief. Each step has two halves:

    \[ \text{predict:}\quad p(x_k\mid z_{1:k-1})=\int p(x_k\mid x_{k-1})\,p(x_{k-1}\mid z_{1:k-1})\,dx_{k-1} \]
    \[ \text{update:}\quad p(x_k\mid z_{1:k})\ \propto\ p(z_k\mid x_k)\,p(x_k\mid z_{1:k-1}) \]

    The prediction pushes the belief through the dynamics (here, the gyro integration), which spreads it out. The update is Bayes' rule with the sensor model as the likelihood, which sharpens it. Two assumptions turn this into a recursion instead of a computation over the whole history: the Markov property (\(x_k\) depends only on \(x_{k-1}\)) and conditional independence of each measurement given the state. Drawn as a Bayes net, the filter is the chain \(x_{k-1}\to x_k\to z_k\), and the missing arcs are exactly those assumptions.

    The Intelligent Systems notes teach every ingredient: Bayes' rule, marginalisation, Bayes nets whose missing arcs encode conditional independence, and the Markov property. They do not teach filtering; the recursion is the extension Extension.

    When every distribution is Gaussian and every model linear, the recursion has a closed form. In one dimension, a Gaussian prior \(\mathcal N(x^-,P^-)\) multiplied by a Gaussian likelihood \(\mathcal N(z,R)\) is another Gaussian, with

    \[ \hat x=x^-+K\,(z-x^-),\qquad K=\frac{P^-}{P^-+R},\qquad P=(1-K)\,P^- \]

    That is the scalar Kalman filter. The gain \(K\) is the fraction of the surprise \(z-x^-\) that you believe: near 1 when the prior is vague (\(P^-\gg R\)), near 0 when the sensor is poor (\(R\gg P^-\)). The posterior variance \(P\) is smaller than both \(P^-\) and \(R\): combining two independent pieces of evidence always helps.

    Two more views help. The Supervised Learning notes show that ridge regression is the maximum a posteriori estimate under a Gaussian prior. The Kalman update minimises the same kind of objective, a measurement misfit weighted by \(R^{-1}\) plus a prior misfit weighted by \((P^-)^{-1}\), so it is ridge-style regularised least squares with the prior covariance as the regulariser Extension. Gaussian noise, variances and covariance matrices, the filter's language, are typical content of an engineering mathematics subject; state observers (a model copy corrected by \(L(y-C\hat x)\)) and the separation principle are typical content of a control systems subject Typical content.

    A Kalman-style attitude filter

    The simulator's Kalman option applies this per axis, with a two-element state: the small attitude error \(\delta\theta\) (rad) and the gyro bias error \(\delta b\) (rad/s). Between star-tracker samples the gyro propagates the quaternion estimate, and the covariance grows:

    \[ \begin{aligned} P^-&=F\,P\,F^\mathsf{T}+Q,\\ F&=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\Delta t\\0&1\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

    \(F\) says the attitude error grows by \(-\Delta t\) times the bias error each step (the bias estimate is subtracted from the measured rate), and \(Q\) holds the gyro noise, \((\sigma_g\Delta t)^2\) on the angle, plus a slow bias random walk. When a star-tracker sample \(q_m\) arrives, the residual is the small rotation from the estimate to the measurement,

    \[ r=2\,\mathrm{vec}\big(\hat q^{*}\otimes q_m\big) \]

    and, with \(H=[1\ \ 0]\) and the measurement variance \(R=\sigma_{st}^2\), the standard update applies to each axis:

    \[ \begin{aligned} K&=P^-H^\mathsf{T}\big(HP^-H^\mathsf{T}+R\big)^{-1}\\ x&=x^-+K\,(z-Hx^-)\\ P&=(I-KH)\,P^- \end{aligned} \]

    The correction is applied multiplicatively: the estimate is turned through the small rotation vector \(\delta\theta\) (the three axes' corrections stacked) and then renormalised,

    \[ \begin{aligned} \hat q&\leftarrow\hat q\otimes\delta q\\ \delta q&=\big[\cos\tfrac{\lVert\delta\theta\rVert}{2},\ \sin\tfrac{\lVert\delta\theta\rVert}{2}\,\hat e\big]\\ &\approx\big[1,\ \tfrac12\delta\theta\big] \end{aligned} \]

    where \(\hat e=\delta\theta/\lVert\delta\theta\rVert\) is the axis of the correction, so the quaternion stays a unit quaternion, and the bias estimate absorbs \(\delta b\). (The half angle is the site's small-rotation convention, \(q\approx[1,\ \theta/2]\), which also put the factor 2 in the residual.) This is the idea of the multiplicative extended Kalman filter (MEKF), the standard spacecraft attitude filter, simplified here to three decoupled axes with no cross-covariance. The simplification is reasonable near rest, and it is stated so that nobody mistakes this filter for the full MEKF Extension.

    Gyro error models, strapdown propagation, Kalman predict and update steps and integrated satellite/inertial navigation are typical content of an aviation navigational systems subject Typical content; the spacecraft filter is the same machine with a star tracker in place of satellite fixes.

    There is a deeper symmetry. In module 05 the LQR gains came from a Riccati equation solved backward in time, as dynamic programming. The covariance recursion above is a Riccati recursion too, run forward in time: the Kalman filter is the dual of the LQR Extension. The Algorithms notes teach dynamic programming and the principle of optimality on discrete problems. Applied to a quadratic value function, the same principle gives LQR, and with it most of the filter's structure Extension.

    Noise meets thresholds

    Now put the filters in the loop. The simulator's sensor extension runs T03 (the safe-mode upset with a fault from 45 to 60 s) with the controller and the mode logic fed the estimate instead of the truth. The requirement checks still use the true state.

    With the default sensors (gyro σ 0.01 deg/s per sample, star tracker σ 0.02° at 5 Hz) Extension, the behaviour is indistinguishable from the true-state run: the same six transitions at nearly the same times. On the T01 slew the estimation error over the last 20 s has an RMS of about 0.008° for the Kalman-style filter, about 0.011° for the complementary filter and about 0.030° for the raw, sample-and-hold star tracker Derived. All three sit far inside every threshold.

    Raise the gyro noise to σ = 0.3 deg/s (preset ESTNOISY, which also raises the star-tracker noise to 0.2°) and the picture changes completely Extension. The noise now sets a floor under the measured rate. The norm of three independent noise components with σ = 0.3 deg/s averages about 0.48 deg/s, and a single sample falls below 0.5 deg/s only about 57% of the time Derived. The SAFE_DETUMBLE exit needs 200 consecutive samples below 0.5 deg/s, and the chance of that is about

    \[ 0.57^{200}\approx10^{-49} \]

    So the craft never leaves SAFE_DETUMBLE Derived. Detumble is pure rate damping, with no attitude term: the initial tumble carries the craft about 46° from the target, and with no return to NOMINAL nothing ever brings it back, so by 120 s the true attitude is about 49° off in the reference run Derived.

    The requirement badges then disagree in an instructive way. REQ-F1 ("stabilize angular rates below 0.5 deg/s after safe-mode entry") passes, because its check uses the true rate, which fell below 0.5 deg/s within a few seconds. REQ-F2 fails, because the craft never returned to NOMINAL. Requirement checks on the truth and mode logic on the estimate can disagree whenever the estimate is worse than the margin built into the thresholds.

    Two remedies follow, and both matter. Filtering: give the mode logic a smoothed rate (averaging \(N\) samples divides white noise by \(\sqrt N\)) or the filter's own rate estimate. Hysteresis: keep the exit threshold well above the noise floor, and require the condition for a time long compared with the noise correlation, which is what the timers of module 08 do.

    Residuals and fault detection

    Where does the project's fault flag come from? In the simulator it is injected: a window from 45 to 60 s in T03 Site default. On a real spacecraft a fault detection, isolation and recovery layer raises it, and estimation is how. The core tool is the residual: compare two quantities that should agree and flag a persistent mismatch. The commanded wheel torque against the measured wheel acceleration; two redundant gyros against each other; the star-tracker attitude against the gyro-propagated one, which is exactly the filter's innovation \(z-Hx^-\).

    The Electromechanics notes describe a hardware residual detector, the residual-current device, which compares the outgoing and returning current and trips on an imbalance. Its blind spot is every residual's blind spot: a fault that affects both signals equally cannot be seen. The Unsupervised Learning notes add why residuals help a detector: an anomaly detector on raw telemetry finds magnitude anomalies, and the residual turns a contextual anomaly (a modest rate that is wrong given zero commanded torque) into a magnitude one it can see Extension. Outlier detection is one of the mining functions listed in the Data Mining notes Extension.

    Rare faults make detection a base-rate problem. Suppose a detector catches 99% of real faults, raises a false alarm on 1% of healthy checks, and the prior probability of a fault at any one check is \(10^{-4}\). Bayes' rule gives

    \[ P(\text{fault}\mid\text{alarm})=\frac{0.99\times10^{-4}}{0.99\times10^{-4}+0.01\times(1-10^{-4})}\approx0.98\% \]

    Ninety-nine alarms in a hundred are false Derived. The Intelligent Systems notes call this the base-rate error: a sensitive test for a rare condition can still give mostly false positives. The cure is more evidence. If consecutive alarms were independent, each one would multiply the odds by the likelihood ratio \(0.99/0.01=99\): two alarms would give about 50%, three about 99% Derived. The project's 0.5 s fault persistence Project is this idea in time: the fault must be present continuously for 50 steps before the mode logic acts. Real false alarms are not independent (a vibration or a noise burst lasts), so persistence works because it outlasts the bursts, not by naive multiplication.

    Redundancy is the other cure. Three independent sensors voting two out of three succeed with probability \(R=3p^2-2p^3\), which is 0.972 at \(p=0.9\) Derived; voting helps only when \(p>0.5\), and it cannot repair a common-mode fault. Module 08 takes the fault flag from here into the mode logic.

      In short: the project assumed perfect knowledge of the state; a real attitude system has to estimate it. A gyro is precise but drifts; an absolute sensor is unbiased but noisy and slow. Fusing them, with a complementary filter or a Kalman-style filter derived from Bayes' rule, bounds the error and learns the bias. With good sensors nothing in the mission changes. With noisy ones the safe-mode exit threshold meets the noise floor and the craft can lock up in SAFE_DETUMBLE, which is why filtering and hysteresis have to be designed together.
