Safe-mode logic and formal reasoning

    Why a safe mode

    Every control law in module 05 assumes the craft is roughly where it should be, turning slowly, with working hardware. When that assumption breaks (a tumble after separation, a stuck wheel, a software fault), the nominal controller can make things worse: an attitude controller fighting a fast tumble saturates every wheel and may never converge. The answer is a safe mode: a rehearsed, conservative state, entered on a defined trigger, whose only job is to bring the vehicle back to a condition from which normal operations can resume. The project's ordering is the classic one: stabilise the rates first, then the attitude Project.

    Aviation is full of the same pattern, and each case below is typical content of the matching subject Typical content:

    • Autorotation. When a helicopter's engine fails, the pilot enters a rehearsed state that trades height for rotor speed. The trigger is defined and the entry is drilled until it is automatic (rotary-wing operations).
    • V1 and the point of no return. Decision thresholds computed in advance turn a continuous, stressful situation into a discrete decision (aircraft performance and planning).
    • Drone failsafes. Losing the command link starts a timer; when it expires the aircraft climbs and returns home, and hysteresis stops a flickering link from toggling the behaviour (remotely piloted aircraft systems).
    • Partial-panel flying. After a vacuum-pump or gyro failure the pilot flies on the remaining, most robust instruments (light aircraft systems). SAFE_DETUMBLE does the same: it uses only the rate, the most robust signal, and the simplest possible law.

    Safety management draws this as a bow-tie around the top event "loss of attitude control": preventive barriers on the left (conservative gains, saturation-aware design, verified software) and recovery barriers on the right (safe-mode entry, detumble, hold) Typical content. A security team's incident response has the same shape: detect, contain, and recover only when recovery is verified Typical content.

    What makes a safe mode trustworthy is that it is exactly specified. The rest of this module treats the project's three-mode machine as a formal object: a set of states, a transition relation, guards that are logical predicates, timers that are integer counters, and invariants that can be proved rather than merely tested.

    The three modes

    The project defines three modes Project. Their control laws come from module 05; the gain values are this site's Site default.

    Modes, their jobs and their control laws
    ModeJobControl lawSite gains
    NOMINALTrack the target attitude.PID or LQR on the error quaternionsee module 05
    SAFE_DETUMBLERemove the rate; ignore pointing.\(\tau=-K_d\,\omega\)\(K_d=\)0.05 N m s/rad
    SAFE_HOLDHold a coarse attitude; block an early return until the fault clears and the craft has been stable for a dwell time.\(\tau=-K_p\,q_{e,v}-K_d\,\omega\) (low-gain PD toward \(q_{ref}\))\(K_p=\)0.01 N m (per unit of quaternion error), \(K_d=\)0.07 N m s/rad

    Two design choices stand out. SAFE_DETUMBLE ignores attitude entirely: it is the rate-damping law whose energy can only fall, whatever the attitude, so it is the safest possible thing to do when nothing else can be trusted. SAFE_HOLD adds a weak attitude term so the craft drifts back toward the target, with low gains to keep torques small and the loop gentle. And the PID integrator runs only in NOMINAL, so a long safe-mode episode cannot wind it up Project.

    Formally the modes are an enumerated set \(M=\{N,\ SD,\ SH\}\). The machine's state, though, is more than the mode: three integer counters (fault time, time below the exit rate, dwell time) carry the history the guards need. Mode plus counters makes it an extended finite-state machine, the same structure module 02 drew as a diagram. The Intelligent Systems notes make the point that matters here: anything not tracked in the state is invisible to every algorithm that runs on it, so without the timers no logic, however clever, could implement hysteresis or dwell.

    Guards, timers and the transition table

    A guard is a predicate that must be true for a transition to fire. The project's three guards are Project:

    \[ \begin{aligned} N\to SD\colon\quad & \lVert\omega\rVert>6^\circ/\text{s}\\ \lor\ \ & t_{\text{fault}}\ge0.5\ \text{s} \end{aligned} \]
    \[ \begin{aligned} SD\to SH\colon\quad & \lVert\omega\rVert<0.5^\circ/\text{s}\\ & \text{continuously for } 2\ \text{s} \end{aligned} \]
    \[ \begin{aligned} SH\to N\colon\quad & \lnot\text{fault}\ \land\ \lVert\omega\rVert<0.5^\circ/\text{s}\\ \land\ \ & e<4^\circ\ \land\ t_{\text{dwell}}\ge8\ \text{s} \end{aligned} \]

    The return guard is a conjunction, so "stay in SAFE_HOLD" is its negation, and De Morgan's law turns that into a disjunction: any one reason is enough to stay.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \lnot G_{SH\to N}=\ & \text{fault}\ \lor\ \lVert\omega\rVert\ge0.5^\circ/\text{s}\\ \lor\ \ & e\ge4^\circ\ \lor\ t_{\text{dwell}}<8\ \text{s} \end{aligned} \]

    Writing the stay condition with "and" instead of "or" is the classic mistake when inverting an if, as the Data Structures notes warn. Sets, relations, predicates and De Morgan's laws are typical content of a discrete structures subject Typical content, and the return guard reads like a checklist in which every item must be ticked; checklists and memory items are typical content of a flight procedures subject Typical content.

    The timers are integer step counters, never accumulated floating-point time. At dt = 0.01 s they count to 50, 200 and 800 steps Derived. The fault counter resets whenever the fault clears, the "below" counter resets on any sample at or above 0.5 deg/s, and the dwell counter starts from zero on entry to SAFE_HOLD. Adding 0.01 to a float until it reaches 8 takes 801 steps, not 800, because 0.01 has no exact binary representation; the counter is exact. Exact definitions matter in hardware too: the Design of Machine Elements notes define bearing failure by a measured spall area, not by "when it feels rough".

    Hysteresis and anti-flapping

    Imagine a single threshold: enter safe mode above 0.5 deg/s, leave below it. A rate hovering near 0.5 deg/s with a little noise would cross the line many times a second, and the craft would switch controllers each time. That is mode flapping, and the project's risk register lists it with a high impact, mitigated by "hysteresis thresholds and minimum dwell times" Project.

    The project uses two defences. The first is a differential gap: enter at 6 deg/s, but leave only below 0.5 deg/s. The second is persistence: the exit condition must hold continuously for 2 s, and the return needs 8 s of dwell. A comparator with a gap is a Schmitt trigger, and the project's logic is a Schmitt trigger with timers. The Electromechanics notes teach exactly this for ON-OFF control: a single threshold chatters under noise, and a differential gap switches off at one level and on at another, trading fewer switches for a wider band. In the terms of the Data Structures notes, the 2 s hold is a sliding-window maximum query, answered in O(1) by a counter that resets on any violation, or by their monotonic deque when the window's peak is wanted for telemetry.

    The timers give a provable bound, the anti-flapping lemma: once the craft leaves NOMINAL it cannot re-enter NOMINAL for at least

    \[ t_{\text{exit hold}}+t_{\text{dwell}}=2\ \text{s}+8\ \text{s}=10\ \text{s} \]

    because SAFE_HOLD can only be entered after 200 consecutive low-rate steps in SAFE_DETUMBLE, and NOMINAL only after 800 dwell steps in SAFE_HOLD, with each counter starting from zero on entry Derived. So there are at most six safe-mode entries in any minute, however the inputs behave.

    The gap and the timers are a trade-off, not free safety. A wider gap or a longer hold means fewer flaps but slower recovery: lowering the exit rate lengthens every detumble, which eats into REQ-L1's 60 s, and a longer dwell delays the return to fine pointing. In the failure vocabulary of the Systems Programming notes, flapping is livelock-like: a liveness failure in which the system keeps moving without making progress. And the structure is not arbitrary. Model mode switching as a small Markov decision process, solved by the value iteration of the Intelligent Systems notes, charge a cost for each switch, and the optimal policy acquires different thresholds for entering and leaving a mode: hysteresis is what an optimal policy looks like when switching costs something.

    Forbidden transitions as invariants

    The project forbids three moves Project: SAFE_DETUMBLE → NOMINAL ("detumbling should pass through safe hold first"), NOMINAL → SAFE_HOLD ("safe hold should only occur after the detumble phase"), and returning to NOMINAL while the fault flag is active. Each is a safety property, a statement that something bad never happens, and temporal logic writes them with \(\mathbf G\) ("globally, at every step") and \(\mathbf X\) ("at the next step"):

    \[ \mathbf G\,\big(\text{mode}=SD\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf X\,\text{mode}\ne N\big) \]
    \[ \mathbf G\,\big(\text{mode}=N\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf X\,\text{mode}\ne SH\big) \]
    \[ \mathbf G\,\big((\text{mode}=SH\land\text{fault})\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf X\,\text{mode}\ne N\big) \]

    The first two are structural, so they live in the transition relation itself. As a boolean adjacency matrix (rows "from", columns "to", in the order N, SD, SH), the forbidden pairs are the zeros off the diagonal:

    \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\1&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

    The machine checks A[from][to] before committing any change. Adjacency matrices and breadth-first search are both in the Computing Algorithms notes: the guard is one O(1) lookup, and a breadth-first search on this graph shows that the shortest path from NOMINAL to SAFE_HOLD has length 2 and must pass through SAFE_DETUMBLE. The proof that no run ever contains a forbidden pair is then an invariant argument by induction over steps. Base: the machine starts in a legal mode. Step: each step either keeps the mode or moves along an edge with \(A=1\), and the assertion stops anything else. So every trace, of any length, is legal. The same pattern proves "the integrator only changes in NOMINAL": it is zero at the start, and the update rule touches it only when the mode is NOMINAL.

    Liveness needs one more fact: every mode can reach NOMINAL. Ignoring self-loops, the only cycle is \(N\to SD\to SH\to N\), so the graph is strongly connected and no mode is a trap. That is necessary but not sufficient: the dynamics must also make each guard come true, which is what the Monte Carlo campaign of module 09 shows. Forbidden transitions and fixed dwell times also make the automation predictable to the people watching it, the human-factors remedy for automation surprise, which is typical content of a human factors subject Typical content.

    Proving vs finding

    An induction proof is only as good as its model of the code. The other way to gain certainty is to check every case, which is possible because the mode logic is finite once the continuous inputs are abstracted. Replace each guard condition by a boolean: rate above 6 deg/s, fault persisted 0.5 s, rate held below 0.5 deg/s for 2 s, fault active, rate below 0.5 deg/s, error below 4°, dwell done. Seven booleans and three modes give

    \[ 3\times2^7=384 \ \text{abstract states} \]

    of which 144 are physically consistent (a rate cannot be both above 6 and below 0.5 deg/s, a 2 s hold below 0.5 deg/s implies the rate is below 0.5 deg/s, and a persisted fault implies an active one) Derived. An exhaustive check visits every one, applies the transition logic, and tests each invariant. If none fails, that is a proof over the abstraction. If one fails, breadth-first search from NOMINAL returns the shortest counterexample, which is the most useful bug report there is.

    Random testing is a different kind of evidence. The 60-trial Monte Carlo campaign samples initial conditions; it can find a bug when a sample happens to hit it, but it can never certify that no bug exists. It is worse than that here: the campaign never injects a fault, so a planted bug that ignores the fault flag in the return guard would pass every Monte Carlo trial ever run Derived. The Intelligent Systems notes put the distinction as complete versus incomplete search: backtracking that answers "no solution" is a proof, while sampling or min-conflicts can only fail to find one. The backtracking pattern of the Algorithms notes, which prunes impossible combinations with a validity test before recursing, covers all 384 combinations by visiting the 144 consistent ones, checking three properties: no forbidden transition, determinism (exactly one successor) and NOMINAL reachable. With the tools of the Python search notes (a dictionary for the transition table, a set of forbidden pairs that raises an exception, and backtracking that assigns, recurses and undoes) such a checker is a few dozen lines.

    The abstraction is what makes this cheap. Keep the real timers instead of booleans and the state space multiplies. At dt = 0.1 s the counters take 21, 6 and 81 values, so the product automaton has \(3\times21\times6\times81=30{,}618\) states; at the simulator's dt = 0.01 s it would be about 24.6 million Derived. That is state explosion in miniature, and the reason real model checkers work so hard on abstraction.

    A run-time monitor

    Proofs cover the design; a monitor covers the run. Log the mode at every tick and the legal logs form a regular language. Under the strict cycle, and starting from NOMINAL, they match

    \[ N^{+}\,\big(SD^{+}\,SH^{+}\,N^{+}\big)^{*}\,\big(SD^{+}\,SH^{*}\big)^{?} \]

    A regular language is recognised by a deterministic finite automaton, so the monitor needs only four states, {N, SD, SH, ERR}, and one table lookup per tick: O(n) time and O(1) memory for a log of n ticks. Its transition function \(\delta\), with the fault rule added:

    Completing the table forces a decision in every cell, and one cell has no answer in the specification: what should happen when the log shows SAFE_HOLD followed by SAFE_DETUMBLE? The monitor rejects it because the spec does not allow it, which leads straight to the next section. In the string-matching terms of the Algorithms notes, the same check is a pattern scan: a forbidden transition is a length-2 pattern, [SD, N] or [N, SH], in consecutive samples, and run-length encoding the log in O(n) lets you assert that every SAFE_HOLD run before a return lasted at least 8 s. The simulator runs this monitor, ADCS.modes.checkTrace, on every preset and every Monte Carlo trial.

    A specification gap

    Three modes give six ordered pairs of different modes. The project's notes cover five of them: three are allowed with guards (N → SD, SD → SH, SH → N) and two are forbidden (SD → N, N → SH) Project. The sixth, SAFE_HOLD → SAFE_DETUMBLE, is not mentioned at all. It is neither allowed nor forbidden; it is simply missing.

    Does it matter? Ask what should happen if the rate rises above 6 deg/s during SAFE_HOLD, say because a wheel fault re-upsets the craft. As written, the machine stays in SAFE_HOLD, and the 8 s dwell timer keeps counting. The hold law's rate gain is in fact higher than detumble's (0.07 against 0.05 N m s/rad), and in the first second it damps the tumble just as fast (3.6 deg/s after 1 s, against 3.8 deg/s for detumble). The difference is its attitude term: once the tumble is gone, it slews the craft back toward the target at up to about 2.8 deg/s, so the rate climbs again and then decays only as fast as the attitude error does. In this site's engine a T03-sized upset (11.2 deg/s, 37° off) that starts in SAFE_HOLD first falls below 0.5 deg/s after about 27 s, against 2.5 s through SAFE_DETUMBLE Derived. The craft still recovers, but its rate stays above 0.5 deg/s about ten times as long. Nobody chose this behaviour; it is what an incomplete table does by default. Finding such holes is a large part of what formal reasoning is for: writing the relation as a complete 3 × 3 table, or completing a DFA's transition function, makes every missing case visible.

    This site implements the specification as written, so its results are the project's. It also offers an option that is off by default, "close the gap", which adds SH → SD with the same trigger as NOMINAL's entry guard, \(\lVert\omega\rVert>6^\circ/\text{s}\lor t_{\text{fault}}\ge0.5\) s Site option.     Where the fault flag comes from

    In the simulator the fault flag is a scripted window, 45 to 60 s in T03 and 10 to 20 s in FAULT Site default. In flight something has to raise it, and the logic above is only as good as that something. Module 07 covers the estimation side; here are the sources and their failure styles.

    • Residual detectors compare two signals that should agree, such as the commanded and the measured wheel torque, and trip on a persistent mismatch, as a residual-current device does in the Electromechanics notes. The same notes describe the layer below software: a motor driver's hardware overcurrent trip is a second, faster and independent protection loop, and the attitude software sees its result as a fault flag.
    • Watchdogs catch the computer itself. The scheduling material of the Database Infrastructure and OS Internals notes explains how a control task misses its deadline, deadlocks or starves; a watchdog that notices becomes an input to the safe-mode supervisor. A watchdog timer that resets the processor into a safe mode is typical content of a computer architecture subject Typical content. The write-ahead logging of the same notes points to a subtle requirement: record the mode change durably before acting on it, or a reset in SAFE_DETUMBLE that reboots into NOMINAL becomes a forbidden SD → N transition through the back door.
    • Persistence as debounce. The 0.5 s persistence is the base-rate defence of module 07: a single noisy sample should not take the craft out of NOMINAL. Telemetry outlier detection with exactly this debounce is the Data Mining link.

    Every detector trades misses against false alarms. Read with the evaluation metrics of the Supervised Learning notes, safe-mode entry is a binary classifier: lowering the threshold or the persistence raises recall (faster, surer detection) and lowers precision (more false entries and lost mission time). As those notes show, the trade is set by the threshold, not by the model. Because a missed fault costs more than a false alarm, \(F_\beta\) with \(\beta>1\) is the natural score. Two results from the agentic-workflow material of the Deep Learning notes carry over as cautions: a verifier's recall sets a ceiling that no retry budget exceeds, so no recovery logic can make up for a fault class the monitor cannot see; and majority voting only amplifies the accuracy its voters already have, so it cannot remove a common-mode fault in which every channel is wrong the same way.

    Finally, the supervisor is what the AI Methodologies notes call a forward-chaining production system, the direction those notes recommend for monitoring: its rules fire on facts (|ω|, fault time, dwell) every step. The same notes name the characteristic weakness of rule-based systems: an unencoded symptom produces no output at all, so symbolic systems fail by silence. Here, a failure that no rule mentions, such as a stuck wheel that keeps |ω| below 6 deg/s and never raises the fault flag, produces no transition at all and looks exactly like a healthy spacecraft. The usual remedies are a catch-all watchdog rule ("enter safe mode unless health is positively confirmed within T") and fault-injection tests for every anticipated fault class.

    Because the mode fact is retracted and replaced at every transition, the rule base is non-monotonic, and the order in which rules are tried becomes part of its meaning. The project's return rule settles one such conflict by construction: it requires "fault clear", so a fault that reappears in the same step as the dwell timer completes keeps the craft in SAFE_HOLD. A rule engine also keeps an audit trail for free: every mode change can be logged with the rule that fired and the fact values that made it fire.
