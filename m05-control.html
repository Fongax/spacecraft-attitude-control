Control design

    The plant

    Every controller is designed against a model of what it controls, the plant. Near the target attitude the rotation is slow and the angles are small. The gyroscopic term \(\omega\times J\omega\) is then second order in \(\omega\): its Jacobian at \(\omega=0\) is zero, so it vanishes from the linearisation, and each axis decouples into a double integrator:

    \[ J_i\,\ddot\theta_i=\tau_i,\qquad G_i(s)=\frac{\Theta_i(s)}{T_i(s)}=\frac{1}{J_i\,s^2} \]

    Torque sets the angular acceleration, which integrates to a rate, which integrates to an angle. There is no natural stiffness pulling the craft back and no natural damping slowing it down: left alone, it keeps whatever rate it has. With \(J_y=0.040\) kg m² Project, a torque of 1 mN m gives 0.025 rad/s² of angular acceleration Derived.

    The first thing to try fails instructively. Proportional-only control, \(\tau=-K\theta\), gives the closed loop

    \[ J s^2+K=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s=\pm j\sqrt{K/J} \]

    Both poles sit on the imaginary axis for every \(K>0\): the craft oscillates for ever about the target with no decay. Rate feedback is mandatory. It also explains why Ziegler–Nichols tuning, which the Electromechanics notes teach for industrial loops, does not fit here. The method raises the gain until the loop just oscillates and reads off that "ultimate" gain and period, but a double integrator under P control oscillates at every gain, so there is no ultimate point to find. Laplace transforms, transfer functions and pole positions are typical content of a control-systems subject Typical content, and Jacobian linearisation of a second calculus subject Typical content. Try the P controller in the step-response lab below to see the undamped oscillation.

    Detumble

    When safe mode has to stop a tumble, attitude does not matter yet; only the rate does. The project's detumble law is pure rate damping Project:

    \[ \tau=-K_d\,\omega,\qquad K_d=0.05\ \text{N m s} \]

    The gain value is a Site default; the notes give the law, not the number.

    A Lyapunov argument proves it removes energy, with no linearisation at all. Take the rotational kinetic energy \(T=\tfrac12\omega^\mathsf{T}J\omega\) as the "energy-like" function and differentiate along Euler's equation:

    \[ \dot T=\omega^\mathsf{T}J\dot\omega=\omega^\mathsf{T}\big(\tau-\omega\times J\omega\big)=\omega^\mathsf{T}\tau=-K_d\,\lVert\omega\rVert^2\le 0 \]

    The gyroscopic term drops out because \(\omega\cdot(\omega\times J\omega)=0\): it moves momentum between axes but does no work. The proof even survives the ±3 mN m torque box Project: a clipped torque \(\tau_i=-\operatorname{sat}(K_d\,\omega_i)\) still has the opposite sign to \(\omega_i\), so \(\omega^\mathsf{T}\tau\) stays negative. The work–energy principle behind this is typical content of a kinematics-and-dynamics subject Typical content.

    Below saturation each axis decays exponentially, \(\omega(t)=\omega_0\,e^{-K_d t/J}\), with a time constant \(J/K_d\) of under a second on every axis; that is the same first-order shape as an R–L or R–C circuit, 63% of the way after one time constant. When \(K_d|\omega|\) exceeds the limit, the torque saturates and the rate falls in a straight line instead, so the minimum braking time is

    \[ t_\text{brake}=\frac{J\,|\omega_0|}{\tau_{max}}\qquad\text{(1.4 s from 6 deg/s on the y axis)} \]

    The 1.4 s is Derived from \(J_y\), the 6 deg/s safe-mode entry threshold and \(\tau_{max}=0.003\) N m.

    A constant disturbance keeps pushing, so the rate does not reach zero: it settles where damping balances the push, inside the ball \(\lVert\omega\rVert\le\lVert\tau_d\rVert/K_d\), here 0.031 deg/s Derived, far below the 0.5 deg/s exit threshold. And rate damping can never fix an attitude: as the Electromechanics notes put it, derivative action alone cannot remove a position error. Detumble leaves the craft pointing wherever it happens to stop, which is why safe mode continues into SAFE_HOLD before fine pointing resumes.

    Stiffness and damping

    To hold an attitude the controller must supply what the plant lacks: a stiffness that pulls the angle back and a damping that stops it overshooting. A proportional-derivative law does both, and the closed loop becomes the familiar mass–spring–damper:

    \[ J\ddot\theta+K_d\dot\theta+K_{p\theta}\theta=0,\qquad \omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{K_{p\theta}}{J}},\qquad \zeta=\frac{K_d}{2\sqrt{K_{p\theta}J}} \]

    The natural frequency \(\omega_n\) (rad/s) sets the speed and the damping ratio \(\zeta\) the shape: \(\zeta<1\) overshoots and rings, \(\zeta=1\) is critically damped, \(\zeta>1\) creeps in without overshoot. For an underdamped loop the 2% settling time is about \(4/(\zeta\omega_n)\). The damped oscillator and these formulas are typical content of a first-year physics subject Typical content.

    One subtlety links this to the project's quaternion law. The controller acts on the error quaternion's vector part, and for small angles \(q_v\approx\theta/2\). A gain \(K_p\) on \(q_v\) is therefore a gain \(K_p/2\) on the angle:

    \[ K_{p\theta}=\tfrac12 K_p \]

    With the site defaults \(K_p=0.02\) and \(K_d=0.08\) Site default, the y axis gets \(\omega_n=0.5\) rad/s and \(\zeta=2.0\) Derived. That is deliberately overdamped, matching the project's description of its gains as "intentionally conservative" Project. The payoff shows in the T01 slew: the rate peaks at 4.32 deg/s Derived and never comes near the 6 deg/s safe-mode threshold.

    Aircraft get stiffness and damping for free: the airflow over the tail pulls the nose back and resists pitching. Static and dynamic stability, and the short-period mode's handling-quality targets in \((\zeta,\omega_n)\), are typical content of aerodynamics subjects Typical content. In orbit there is no air, so \(K_p\) and \(K_d\) must supply both Analogy.

    Integral action and windup

    A PD loop holds the target only if nothing pushes. A constant disturbance torque \(\tau_d\) must be balanced by a constant control torque, and a PD law can only produce one from a constant error. The final value theorem gives that error:

    \[ \theta_{ss}=\frac{\tau_d}{K_{p\theta}} \]

    With the project disturbance \([2,\,-1,\,1.5]\times10^{-5}\) N m Project and the site gains, that is about 0.15° of attitude error Derived: inside the 2° requirement, but permanent. Adding an integral term, which turns PD into PID, lets the controller build up exactly the torque the disturbance needs while the error goes to zero. Steady-state error and disturbance rejection are typical content of a control-systems subject Typical content; the Electromechanics notes cover the same P, I and D actions on a motor rig, including "a P controller can't hold a load at zero error".

    The integral adds a third pole, so stability is no longer automatic. The characteristic equation is a cubic and the Routh–Hurwitz test gives the condition:

    \[ J s^3+K_d s^2+K_{p\theta}s+K_{i\theta}=0\quad\text{is stable}\iff K_d K_{p\theta}>J K_{i\theta} \]

    With the site gains on the y axis: \(K_dK_{p\theta}=8\times10^{-4}\) against \(JK_{i\theta}=4\times10^{-5}\), a factor of 20 to spare Derived.

    The integral's weakness is windup. During a large slew the error stays big for a long time and the integrator keeps charging; by the time the craft reaches the target, the stored integral pushes it straight past. On the 37° T01 slew with the site default \(K_i=0.002\), the clamped integrator settles in 18.3 s; without the clamp it takes between 38 and 68 s for any \(K_i\) from 0.0005 to 0.01 Derived. The anti-windup options:

    • Mode gating: integrate only in NOMINAL, the project's choice Project.
    • Clamping: bound the integral term, \(|K_i I|\le 0.1\,\tau_{max}\), this site's addition Site default.
    • Conditional integration: freeze the integrator while the actuator is saturated.
    • Reset on entry: zero the integrator when the controller is re-enabled.

    The Electromechanics notes name the same fixes. A machine-learning echo: an LSTM cell is a gated integrator, and its input gate plays the role of anti-windup, while a leaky integrator cannot remove a steady error Analogy.

    The quaternion-error PID

    The project's nominal controller works directly on quaternions Project. It first forms the error quaternion, the rotation from the reference to the current attitude, flipping its sign if needed so that it describes the shorter way round, and then applies PID to its vector part:

    \[ q_e=q_{ref}^{*}\otimes q\ \ (\text{negate if } q_{e,0}<0),\qquad \tau=-K_p\,q_{e,v}-K_d\,\omega-K_i\!\int q_{e,v}\,dt \]

    For small errors \(q_{e,v}\approx\theta/2\) on each axis, so every single-axis result above carries over with \(K_{p\theta}=K_p/2\). For large errors the law stays sensible: \(|q_{e,v}|=\sin(\theta_e/2)\) never exceeds 1, so the proportional torque is bounded even at 180°.

    Notice what multiplies \(K_d\): the measured body rate \(\omega\), not the time derivative of the error. This is derivative on measurement. When the reference attitude steps, the error jumps but \(\omega\) does not, so there is no spike of torque, the "derivative kick" that the Electromechanics notes warn about. The integral accumulates only in NOMINAL Project, and this site also clamps it Site default. In code, one control step is short:

    let qe = quat.mul(quat.conj(qRef), q);          // error quaternion
if (qe[0] < 0) qe = qe.map(c => -c);              // shortest rotation
const qv = [qe[1], qe[2], qe[3]];
for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
  tauCmd[i] = -Kp * qv[i] - Kd * w[i] - Ki * I[i];  // derivative on measurement
  if (mode === NOMINAL) I[i] = clamp(I[i] + qv[i] * dt, -Imax, Imax);
}
const tau = tauCmd.map(t => clamp(t, -tauMax, tauMax));   // per-axis saturation

    Hobby and professional drone flight controllers use the same structure, an angle or quaternion-error PID with anti-windup and output limits, nested inside a faster rate loop; flight controllers are typical content of a remotely piloted aircraft subject Typical content.

    LQR

    PID gains are chosen by feel. The linear-quadratic regulator chooses them by optimisation: state what you care about as a cost and let mathematics find the best linear feedback. The project designed its LQR on the small-angle state \(x=[\theta,\ \omega]\), with \(\theta\approx2q_{e,v}\) Project, and the cost

    \[ \mathcal{J}=\int_0^\infty\big(x^\mathsf{T}Qx+u^\mathsf{T}Ru\big)\,dt,\qquad Q=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,2,\,0.15,0.15,0.15),\quad R=2000\,I \]

    \(Q\) and \(R\) are the project's weights Project. A large \(R\) says "torque is expensive", which keeps the gains gentle.

    The optimal law is \(u=-Kx\) with \(K=R^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P\), where \(P\) solves the continuous algebraic Riccati equation

    \[ A^\mathsf{T}P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P+Q=0,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\ B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1/J_i\end{bmatrix}\ \text{per axis} \]

    Because \(J\), \(Q\) and \(R\) are all diagonal, the 6-state problem splits into three independent 2-state problems, and each has a closed-form answer:

    \[ K_\theta=\sqrt{\frac{q_1}{r}}=0.0316\ \tfrac{\text{N m}}{\text{rad}},\qquad K_\omega=\sqrt{\frac{2J_i\sqrt{q_1r}+q_2}{r}}=0.0478,\ 0.0510,\ 0.0407\ \tfrac{\text{N m s}}{\text{rad}} \]

    The attitude gain is the same on every axis and the rate gain grows with inertia. The closed loops have \(\omega_n=0.95,\ 0.89,\ 1.12\) rad/s and \(\zeta\approx0.72\) Derived: a classic, lightly underdamped design. In the T01/T02 comparison it settles in 5.39 s against the site PID's 18.33 s Derived. The y-axis poles sit near \(s\approx-0.64\pm0.62j\); on x they are \(-0.68\pm0.66j\) Derived. The project also stored a fallback gain matrix so that it could run without a control toolbox Project; this site always computes the gains from \(Q\) and \(R\).

    The whole design rests on \(\theta\approx2q_{e,v}\). Since \(2\sin(\theta/2)\) replaces \(\theta\), the approximation is 0.8% off at 25° and 17% off at 120° Derived: good near the target, poor in a big slew. Navigators know the same trade as the 1-in-60 rule, typical content of a navigation subject Typical content. State-space feedback \(u=-Kx\) and pole placement are typical content of a control-systems subject Typical content; LQR is the optimal-control step beyond them.

    LQR as dynamic programming

    There is a second way to reach the same gain, and it connects control to planning and reinforcement learning. It works in discrete time, so first sample one axis with the torque held over each step of length \(\Delta t\). The double integrator then moves exactly as

    \[ \begin{gathered} x_{k+1}=A_d\,x_k+B_d\,u_k,\\[4pt] A_d=\begin{bmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad B_d=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta t^2/(2J_i)\\ \Delta t/J_i\end{bmatrix} \end{gathered} \]

    \(A_d\) and \(B_d\) replace the continuous \(A\) and \(B\) above. The cost is charged per step, each step paying its share of the integral:

    \[ \begin{gathered} Q_d=Q\,\Delta t,\qquad R_d=R\,\Delta t,\\[2pt] Q=\operatorname{diag}(2,\ 0.15),\quad R=2000 \end{gathered} \]

    The per-axis weights are the project's Project.

    Call \(V(x)\) the cost-to-go: the least total cost from state \(x\) onwards. Bellman's principle says an optimal path is optimal from every point along it, so one step of cost plus the cost-to-go from wherever that step lands must be minimised:

    \[ V(x)=\min_u\Big[x^\mathsf{T}Q_d\,x+u^\mathsf{T}R_d\,u+V\big(A_dx+B_du\big)\Big] \]

    For linear dynamics and quadratic cost, a quadratic guess \(V(x)=x^\mathsf{T}Px\) stays quadratic after the minimisation, and repeating the step from \(P_0=0\) is exactly the discrete Riccati recursion:

    \[ \begin{aligned} K_k&=\big(R_d+B_d^\mathsf{T}P_kB_d\big)^{-1}B_d^\mathsf{T}P_kA_d,\\ P_{k+1}&=Q_d+A_d^\mathsf{T}P_k\big(A_d-B_dK_k\big) \end{aligned} \]

    Updating \(P\) is value iteration; reading off \(K_k\), the greedy action for the current \(V\), is policy extraction. Markov decision processes, Bellman equations, value iteration and policy extraction are all in the Intelligent Systems notes, and the Algorithms notes cover dynamic programming: the backward Riccati sweep is a table filled in from the horizon. LQR is the rare case where the Bellman equation has an exact solution, which makes it the provable-optimum baseline any learned controller, such as the Q-learning and actor-critic methods in the reinforcement-learning notes, should be measured against.

And the discrete answer sits a little below the continuous one because a 0.1 s step is coarse; at \(\Delta t=0.01\) s the sweep converges to \([0.03142,\ 0.05087]\), within 1% of the closed form Derived.     When linear analysis stops

    Everything above assumed the actuator delivers whatever torque the law asks for. It cannot: each axis is limited to ±0.003 N m Project. The LQR attitude term alone reaches that limit at

    \[ K_\theta\,|\theta|=\tau_{max}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\theta|=\frac{\tau_{max}}{K_\theta}=\frac{0.003}{0.0316}\ \text{rad}=5.44^\circ \]

    Beyond about 5.4° Derived the loop is no longer linear, and \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) stop describing it. In the 37° T02 slew the actuator sits at its limit and accelerates the craft for longer than the linear design expects, so the rate peaks at 14.28 deg/s, a margin of only 0.72 deg/s to the 15 deg/s limit of REQ-S1 Derived. Switch safe mode on (preset LQRSAFE) and that rate trips the 6 deg/s entry threshold at 0.64 s Derived: the linear design and the mode logic collide. The conservative PID peaks at 4.32 deg/s on the same slew Derived.

    What is the best possible slew under a hard torque limit? Accelerate at full torque for half the angle and brake at full torque for the other half, a bang-bang manoeuvre. For a rest-to-rest rotation by \(\Phi\):

    \[ t_{min}=2\sqrt{\frac{\Phi\,J}{\tau_{max}}}\qquad(30^\circ\ \text{on the y axis in about 5.3 s}) \]

    The 5.3 s is Derived. It is a lower bound no linear controller can beat, and a useful yardstick for the settling times in the next section. Saturation also bends the torque vector when several axes clip at once; module 06 shows how. Machine-design practice treats a limit like this as a designed, predictable feature. Backlash and friction are the nonlinearities to watch next, because with integral action they can make a loop limit-cycle. TCP congestion control offers a parallel from the Networking notes: the sender's rate is the minimum of two windows, a saturation in a delayed feedback loop Analogy.

    Comparing controllers

    Which controller is better depends on what you value, so the project compared them on five criteria Project:

    • Settling time: the first time after which attitude error stays below 2° and rate below 0.5 deg/s.
    • Peak error: the largest attitude error during the run.
    • Maximum torque: the largest actuator command after saturation.
    • Control effort: the integral of squared torque, \(\int\lVert\tau\rVert^2dt\), in N² m² s.
    • Safety margin: the distance from the 15 deg/s rate limit.

    The project's verdict was qualitative: LQR is "cleaner near the reference attitude", while PID is "more intuitive and easier to tune but requires anti-windup protection" Project. A weighted decision matrix makes such a verdict explicit: score each option on each criterion, weight the criteria by what the mission values, and add up.       In short: each axis is a double integrator that needs both stiffness and damping from the controller. Detumble removes energy by construction; PID adds stiffness and an integral that must be protected from windup; LQR derives its gains from a cost, by Riccati or by Bellman's value iteration. The ±3 mN m limit ends the linear story above about 5.4° of error, and which controller is "best" depends on the weights you give the project's five criteria.
